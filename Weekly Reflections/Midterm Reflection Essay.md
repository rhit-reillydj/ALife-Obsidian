### Introduction

In this course, we are studying the field of Artificial Life. Artificial Life is an attempt to create "life" in an artificial way, most commonly through computers. This begs the question, "What do we mean by life?". Different fields address this question in different ways, and Artificial Life is a developing and broad field that looks to create life in accordance with any of these definitions. 

For example, when an organism is intelligent, it often seems to be alive to an outside observer. This phenomena can be seen in Braitenberg vehicles. Little robots that either move toward or away from light, a very simple form of intelligence. To an observer, this could seem like emotion or survival instinct, but it is actually very simple sensors with a minimal neural network. So is this, or even a more complicated form of AI such as LLMs considered to be alive? I do not believe so.

### What is "Life"?

Through this course, there are a few key abilities that resonate with me and make something seem alive. The abilities: adaptation, growth, reproduction, and homeostasis. This is not a comprehensive list, I am no expert, and my understanding of life continues to evolve as the course progresses, but these traits seem to be at the core of what Artificial Life tries to emulate. Systems like [[Week 4 Reflections|evolutionary algorithms]] or [[Week 4 Reflections|self-replicating systems]] utilize adaptation and reproduction, but individual organisms do not have many unique experiences. Their environments are unchanging and they do not grow or change after they have been produced by their parents. Developmental systems utilize growth and homeostasis, but do not often have to adapt or reproduce. It seems as if an artificial life simulation that included all of these abilities would truly be a creation of "life", then?

Many people outside of the artificial life community would likely not consider ALife simulations to truly be alive. They might say that the organisms need to eat, be made of cells, or be carbon-based to truly be alive. Their definition of life is deeply linked to how they have experienced and witnessed life here on Earth. But artificial life is often unconcerned with what life *is* here on Earth, but rather how life *could be*. This distinction is one of my biggest takeaways from this course.

### Simulation

Despite our goal of understanding life as it could be, we have a wonderful starting point and source of inspiration. Life on Earth. There is no need to reinvent the wheel, and mother nature has invented a mighty fine wheel herself. In Alife, we take inspiration from evolution, cellular biology, chemistry, neural systems, and much more. We often attempt to recreate these real world phenomena in simulation. Sometimes in an attempt to accurately recreate the phenomenon, and sometimes in an attempt to witness how the phenomenon could interact with un-earthly environments (organisms without cells, carbon, or real-world physics). 

This leads to a divide in the ALife community on the purpose of simulation. There are often two schools of thought: Simulations are accurate recreations of real-world events and should be studies as such. Simulations are simply thought experiments that do not have many real implications. In *[[Simulations as Opaque Thought Experiments]]*, Di Paolo et al. argues that simulations are a combination of the two. They offer interesting thought experiments that have real-world implications that must be studied and explained. Creating the simulation and observing emergent results is not enough. One must study these systems to explain the emergent behaviors and understand what is revealed about our world.

### [[Week 1 Reflections - The Science of ALIFE|Emergence]]

A topic that is core to artificial life and artificial intelligence is emergence. Emergence is the idea that complicated outcomes can emerge from a large quantity of simple interactions. "The whole is greater than the sum of the parts." I believe that these simple interactions are necessary for life to exist. Life *cannot* exist without emergence. Even single-celled organisms exhibit emergent properties from all of their atoms interacting with each other. Emergence can be seen in many aspects of artificial life and intelligence. Many cells make up an organism, many neurons make up a neural network, and many organisms make up a [[Swarm Systems as a Platform for Open-Ended Evolutionary Dynamics||Swarm System]]. 

It is almost impossible to design life piece by piece, as emergence is too complicated and interconnected for us to understand, much less design. Instead, we must act as facilitators for life and its properties to emerge through many small interactions. We must create systems that allow for these small interactions to occur, interact, and change through time.

### Implications for My Field

I am studying and plan to devote my life to the field of Artificial Intelligence. AI and ALife are very closely related and have a lot of overlap, drawing from and inspiring each other. Neural networks, evolutionary systems, swarm systems, and reinforcement learning are all core tools used in both fields, and are all inspired by life on Earth.

An interesting byproduct of creating life is that you often create intelligence along the way. It is difficult to create a population of organisms capable of the complexities of life without intelligence. This leads me to believe that intelligence is necessary for life, but life is not necessary for intelligence.
